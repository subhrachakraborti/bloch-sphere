Bloch Sphere Foundations

Understand qubits, rotations, and quantum gates.

The Bloch Sphere is the most intuitive way to visualize a single qubit. It represents every pure quantum state as a point on a unit sphere. This guide pairs the geometry with the math behind quantum gates so you can read and design qubit transformations.

What is the Bloch Sphere?

A qubit state can be written as |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^{iφ} sin(θ/2)|1⟩. The angles θ and φ locate the state on the surface of a unit sphere. The north pole is |0⟩, the south pole is |1⟩, and the equator contains equal superpositions. The global phase is not observable, so each physical state maps to a unique point on the sphere.

The Bloch vector (x, y, z) is given by x = sin θ cos φ, y = sin θ sin φ, z = cos θ. Visualizing gates as rotations around the axes makes it easier to reason about quantum algorithms.

Core Quantum Gates

Single-qubit gates are 2×2 unitary matrices. They rotate the state vector around the Bloch Sphere without changing its length.

  • Pauli-X: flips |0⟩ and |1⟩ (rotation about the x-axis).
  • Pauli-Y: rotates around the y-axis with a phase shift.
  • Pauli-Z: adds a phase to |1⟩ (rotation about the z-axis).
  • Hadamard: maps |0⟩ to |+⟩ and |1⟩ to |−⟩ (rotation between z and x).
  • Phase (S, T): fractional rotations around the z-axis.

How Gates Rotate the Sphere

Gates correspond to rotations of the Bloch vector: X rotates 180° around the x-axis, Y rotates 180° around the y-axis, and Z rotates 180° around the z-axis. The Hadamard gate rotates the state about the axis (x + z)/√2, swapping the z- and x-axes. Phase gates S and T rotate about the z-axis by 90° and 45° respectively.

Because the state vector stays on the sphere surface, pure states always have a length of 1. Any decoherence or measurement would move the state inward toward the center, representing a mixed state.

Measurement Probabilities

Measuring in the computational basis yields P(|0⟩) = |α|² and P(|1⟩) = |β|². In the x-basis (|+⟩, |−⟩), probabilities depend on the projection onto the x-axis. The visualization shows these outcomes directly from the state vector so you can compare geometry with the math.

Beginner Tips

  • Start at |0⟩ and apply X, Y, Z to see axis rotations.
  • Use Hadamard to create equal superpositions on the equator.
  • Phase gates spin the vector around the vertical axis without changing probabilities.
  • Adjust θ and φ to see how amplitudes and phases change together.
